01 — PHILOSOPHY

Philosophy

The study of questions that sit underneath almost everything else: What is real? What can we know? What makes something right or wrong? What makes a life meaningful? And how should we live?

Philosophy begins when you stop accepting an answer simply because it has always been given.

01 — HISTORY

Follow the timeline

Philosophical traditions did not appear in isolation. Ideas developed in response to earlier arguments, political changes, scientific discoveries, religious traditions, and changing understandings of humanity.

c. 600–300 BCE

Ancient Greek philosophy

Early Greek thinkers shifted attention toward reasoned explanations of nature, knowledge, reality, and human conduct. Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle became especially influential.

c. 300 BCE–300 CE

Hellenistic philosophy

Stoicism, Epicureanism, Skepticism, and related traditions focused heavily on how people should live, respond to uncertainty, and pursue a good life.

c. 400–1400

Medieval philosophy

Philosophers across Christian, Islamic, and Jewish traditions explored relationships among reason, revelation, metaphysics, ethics, and the nature of God.

c. 1500–1700

Renaissance and early modern thought

Humanism, political philosophy, scientific developments, and new approaches to knowledge transformed European intellectual life.

c. 1600–1800

Rationalism and empiricism

Thinkers debated the foundations of knowledge. Rationalists emphasized reason, while empiricists placed greater emphasis on experience and observation.

c. 1700–1800

The Enlightenment

Philosophers examined reason, liberty, political authority, toleration, rights, and the structure of society.

c. 1800–1900

German idealism and 19th-century philosophy

Kant, Hegel, Marx, Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, and others transformed debates about history, consciousness, society, religion, morality, and freedom.

c. 1900–present

Contemporary philosophy

Analytic philosophy, existentialism, phenomenology, pragmatism, critical theory, philosophy of mind, philosophy of language, political philosophy, ethics, and many other fields continue to develop.

02 — THINKERS

Meet the thinkers

Start with influential philosophers, then follow their arguments into the wider traditions surrounding them.

c. 470–399 BCE

Socrates

ANCIENT GREEK PHILOSOPHY

Known for questioning assumptions rather than simply presenting doctrines. The Socratic method uses questioning and dialogue to examine claims, definitions, and beliefs.

FAMOUS “The unexamined life is not worth living.”
LESSER-KNOWN “I know that I am intelligent because I know that I know nothing.”

c. 428–348 BCE

Plato

PLATONISM

Student of Socrates and teacher of Aristotle. Plato explored knowledge, justice, education, reality, politics, and the relationship between appearances and deeper forms of reality.

FAMOUS “Opinion is the medium between knowledge and ignorance.”
LESSER-KNOWN “The beginning is the most important part of the work.”
Save to Notes
384–322 BCE

Aristotle

ARISTOTELIANISM

Developed systematic approaches to logic, ethics, politics, metaphysics, biology, and natural philosophy. His virtue ethics emphasizes character and human flourishing.

FAMOUS “We are what we repeatedly do.”
LESSER-KNOWN “It is the mark of an educated mind to be able to entertain a thought without accepting it.”
Save to Notes
c. 50–135 CE

Epictetus

STOICISM

A major Stoic teacher who emphasized distinguishing between what is within our control and what is not, while developing character through disciplined judgment.

FAMOUS “It is not things that trouble us, but our judgments about things.”
LESSER-KNOWN “No man is free who is not master of himself.”
Save to Notes
1596–1650

René Descartes

RATIONALISM

Used methodological doubt to investigate what could be known with certainty. His work shaped modern epistemology, metaphysics, and philosophy of mind.

FAMOUS “I think, therefore I am.”
LESSER-KNOWN “The only secure knowledge is that which cannot be doubted.”
Save to Notes
1632–1704

John Locke

EMPIRICISM

Argued that knowledge is closely connected to experience. His political philosophy developed influential ideas about natural rights, consent, and legitimate government.

FAMOUS “Where there is no law, there is no freedom.”
LESSER-KNOWN “New opinions are always suspected, and usually opposed.”
Save to Notes
1724–1804

Immanuel Kant

CRITICAL PHILOSOPHY

Investigated the conditions that make knowledge, morality, and judgment possible. His moral philosophy emphasizes rational principles and treating persons as ends rather than merely as means.

FAMOUS “Dare to know.”
LESSER-KNOWN “Science is organized knowledge. Wisdom is organized life.”
Save to Notes
1844–1900

Friedrich Nietzsche

19TH-CENTURY PHILOSOPHY

Critiqued traditional morality, religion, cultural assumptions, and concepts of truth. His writings explore value creation, self-overcoming, power, and the problem of nihilism.

FAMOUS “He who has a why to live can bear almost any how.”
LESSER-KNOWN “There are no facts, only interpretations.”
Save to Notes
1813–1855

Søren Kierkegaard

EXISTENTIALISM — PRECURSOR

Examined anxiety, faith, individuality, choice, responsibility, and the tension between objective systems and lived human existence.

FAMOUS “Life can only be understood backwards; but it must be lived forwards.”
LESSER-KNOWN “To dare is to lose one's footing momentarily. Not to dare is to lose oneself.”

03 — PROFILE MAP

Follow the questions

Philosophers often become easier to understand when you follow the problem they were trying to solve.

Socrates

KNOWLEDGE · ETHICS · QUESTIONING

CENTRAL QUESTION

How can we know whether what we believe is actually true?

KEY IDEAS

  • Socratic questioning
  • Intellectual humility
  • Ethical self-examination
  • Definition and argument

Plato

REALITY · KNOWLEDGE · JUSTICE · POLITICS

CENTRAL QUESTION

What is ultimately real, and how should that understanding shape the way society is organized?

KEY IDEAS

  • Forms
  • Allegory of the Cave
  • Philosopher-rulers
  • Justice

Aristotle

VIRTUE · LOGIC · POLITICS · FLOURISHING

CENTRAL QUESTION

What does it mean for a human being to live well and flourish?

KEY IDEAS

  • Virtue ethics
  • The golden mean
  • Eudaimonia
  • Formal logic

John Locke

KNOWLEDGE · RIGHTS · GOVERNMENT · CONSENT

CENTRAL QUESTION

What gives government legitimate authority over individuals?

KEY IDEAS

  • Natural rights
  • Consent of the governed
  • Limited government
  • Empiricism
04 — TRADITIONS

Explore schools of thought

A school of thought is a broad intellectual tradition. Philosophers within the same tradition can disagree substantially, so treat these as maps rather than boxes.

ANCIENT GREECE

Stoicism

Focuses on virtue, rational judgment, and distinguishing what is within our control from what is outside it.

ANCIENT GREECE

Epicureanism

Connects the good life with tranquility, friendship, moderation, and freedom from unnecessary fear and disturbance.

ANCIENT GREECE

Platonism

Associated with Plato's account of Forms, knowledge, reality, justice, and the education of the soul.

EARLY MODERN

Rationalism

Gives reason and rational insight a central role in explaining knowledge and reality.

EARLY MODERN

Empiricism

Emphasizes experience and observation as important foundations for knowledge.

19TH–20TH CENTURY

Existentialism

Examines freedom, responsibility, meaning, individuality, anxiety, and the conditions of human existence.

19TH–20TH CENTURY

Pragmatism

Evaluates ideas partly through their practical consequences, uses, and relationships to inquiry and experience.

20TH CENTURY

Phenomenology

Investigates conscious experience and the structures through which things are experienced and understood.

20TH CENTURY

Analytic Philosophy

Places strong emphasis on clarity, argument, logic, language, and careful analysis of concepts.

19TH–20TH CENTURY

Marxism

Examines class, labor, economic organization, ideology, power, and historical change.

20TH CENTURY

Critical Theory

Investigates social structures, ideology, power, domination, culture, and possibilities for social transformation.

CROSS-TRADITIONAL

Skepticism

Questions whether, and to what extent, certain kinds of knowledge or certainty are possible.

05 — GLOSSARY

Learn the concepts

Philosophy becomes much easier to navigate once its recurring vocabulary becomes familiar.

Epistemology

The branch of philosophy concerned with knowledge, belief, justification, and the limits of knowing.

Metaphysics

The study of fundamental questions about reality, existence, identity, causation, and what ultimately exists.

Ethics

The philosophical study of morality, including questions about right action, wrong action, virtue, and the good life.

Logic

The study of valid reasoning and the structures through which arguments succeed or fail.

Ontology

The study of being and existence: what kinds of things exist and what it means for something to be.

Nihilism

A family of positions associated with the rejection or absence of objective meaning, value, or purpose.

Determinism

The view that events, including human actions, arise from prior conditions according to causal necessity.

Free Will

The capacity or possibility of agents to choose and act freely, especially in relation to competing theories of causation.

Virtue

A stable excellence of character or disposition that contributes to good action and flourishing.

Rationalism

An approach that gives reason a major role in explaining knowledge and discovering truths.

Empiricism

The view that experience and observation are fundamental sources of knowledge.

Social Contract

A family of theories explaining political authority through some form of agreement, consent, or mutual justification among people.

06 — INQUIRY

Ask a question

Don't begin with “What is the answer?” Begin by turning your curiosity into a question that can actually be investigated.

Your question

Start here:
  1. Define the important terms in your question.
  2. Find at least two philosophers who disagree.
  3. Identify the strongest argument on each side.
  4. Look for objections to both arguments.
  5. Write your own provisional conclusion.
