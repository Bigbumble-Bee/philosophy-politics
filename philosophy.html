Timeline

HOW THE QUESTIONS CHANGED

Philosophy does not develop as a straight line where one thinker simply replaces another. Ideas overlap, return, disagree, and transform. This timeline gives you the larger map.

c. 600–300 BCE

Ancient Greek Philosophy

Greek philosophers began asking systematic questions about nature, reality, knowledge, justice, virtue, and the good life. Philosophy gradually shifted from explanations based primarily on myth toward rational argument and investigation.

Thinkers Thales, Heraclitus, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle
Questions What is reality? What is virtue? What is justice?
Legacy Logic, ethics, metaphysics, political philosophy
c. 300 BCE–300 CE

Hellenistic Philosophy

After Alexander the Great, philosophy increasingly focused on how a person should live in an unstable world. Different schools offered competing answers about happiness, virtue, pleasure, reason, and emotional control.

Schools Stoicism, Epicureanism, Skepticism
Questions How should I live? What can I control?
Legacy Ethics, psychology, theories of happiness
c. 400–1400

Medieval Philosophy

Philosophers in Christian, Islamic, and Jewish intellectual traditions explored relationships between reason, revelation, God, morality, existence, and knowledge. Ancient Greek works were preserved, interpreted, challenged, and expanded.

Thinkers Augustine, Avicenna, Maimonides, Aquinas
Questions Can reason establish truths about God?
Legacy Natural law, metaphysics, theology, logic
c. 1400–1700

Renaissance & Early Modern Thought

European intellectual life experienced major changes in politics, science, religion, art, and education. Philosophers increasingly examined the individual, political authority, scientific knowledge, and the foundations of certainty.

Thinkers Machiavelli, Bacon, Descartes, Hobbes, Locke
Questions How can we know what is true?
Legacy Modern science, liberalism, social contract theory
c. 1700–1800

The Enlightenment

Enlightenment thinkers emphasized reason, criticism of inherited authority, individual rights, political liberty, religious toleration, and the possibility of social progress.

Thinkers Locke, Hume, Rousseau, Voltaire, Kant
Questions What makes political authority legitimate?
Legacy Rights, democracy, liberalism, modern political thought
1800s

Nineteenth-Century Philosophy

Industrialization, nationalism, capitalism, revolution, religion, and changing ideas about the individual transformed philosophical questions. Thinkers increasingly challenged established moral, political, and religious frameworks.

Thinkers Hegel, Marx, Kierkegaard, Mill, Nietzsche
Questions What shapes human freedom and society?
Legacy Socialism, existentialism, feminism, modern critique
1900s–Present

Contemporary Philosophy

Philosophy became increasingly diverse. Analytic philosophy emphasized language, logic, and precise argument, while continental traditions explored existence, interpretation, society, power, meaning, and lived experience. Pragmatism, feminism, philosophy of mind, political philosophy, and philosophy of science continue to develop.

Thinkers Russell, Wittgenstein, Arendt, Beauvoir, Rawls
Questions What are language, identity, power, and consciousness?
Legacy Modern ethics, politics, mind, language, science

IDEAS IN CONTEXT

Socrates

c. 470–399 BCE

Who was he?

An Athenian philosopher famous for questioning conventional assumptions about virtue, knowledge, justice, and how a person should live.

Central idea

A life should be examined rather than simply accepted. Socratic questioning attempts to uncover contradictions and clarify what a person actually believes.

Method

The Socratic method uses disciplined questioning to test definitions, assumptions, and arguments.

“The unexamined life is not worth living.”

Attributed to Socrates in Plato's Apology.

Connections

Ethics Knowledge Plato Socratic Method

Plato

c. 428–348 BCE

Who was he?

A student of Socrates and teacher of Aristotle. Plato founded the Academy and wrote philosophical dialogues exploring knowledge, justice, politics, education, love, and reality.

Central idea

Plato argued that the visible world is not the highest level of reality. His theory of Forms describes unchanging realities underlying changing particular things.

Political philosophy

In the Republic, Plato investigates justice and proposes a political order governed by people trained in philosophy.

“The beginning is the most important part of the work.”

Attributed to Plato in the Republic.

Connections

Forms Justice Politics Socrates Aristotle

Aristotle

384–322 BCE

Who was he?

A student of Plato who developed influential theories across logic, ethics, metaphysics, politics, biology, rhetoric, and natural philosophy.

Virtue ethics

Aristotle argued that living well requires developing excellent habits and practical wisdom. Virtue is cultivated through action rather than simply memorizing moral rules.

The good life

Human flourishing, or eudaimonia, is the central aim of Aristotle's ethical thought.

“We are what we repeatedly do.”

Commonly attributed to Aristotle; the wording is a later paraphrase rather than a direct quotation.

Connections

Virtue Ethics Logic Politics Plato

René Descartes

1596–1650

Who was he?

A French philosopher and mathematician whose work helped establish modern philosophical rationalism.

Method of doubt

Descartes deliberately doubted beliefs that could possibly be mistaken in order to determine whether anything could be known with certainty.

Mind and body

His philosophy distinguished thinking substance from extended physical substance, contributing to the enduring mind-body problem.

“I think, therefore I am.”

From Descartes' Meditations and related works.

Connections

Rationalism Skepticism Mind Knowledge

John Locke

1632–1704

Who was he?

An English philosopher whose work strongly influenced empiricism and modern political philosophy.

Knowledge

Locke argued that human knowledge is connected to experience, challenging theories that placed innate ideas at the foundation of knowledge.

Political philosophy

Locke defended natural rights, government by consent, and the idea that political authority is limited by the rights of individuals.

“Where there is no property, there is no injustice.”

John Locke, Second Treatise of Government.

Connections

Immanuel Kant

1724–1804

Who was he?

A German Enlightenment philosopher who attempted to explain how reason and experience work together in human knowledge.

Ethics

Kant's deontological ethics emphasizes duty, rational moral principles, and treating people as ends rather than merely as means.

Enlightenment

Kant described enlightenment as humanity's emergence from self-imposed intellectual dependence.

“Dare to know!”

Immanuel Kant, “What Is Enlightenment?”

Connections

Enlightenment Ethics Reason Knowledge

Friedrich Nietzsche

1844–1900

Who was he?

A German philosopher and cultural critic who challenged traditional assumptions about morality, religion, truth, and human values.

Critique of morality

Nietzsche investigated where moral values come from and whether inherited moral systems actually promote human flourishing.

Meaning

Nietzsche's work confronts the problem of creating meaning and values in a world where traditional sources of certainty have weakened.

“He who has a why to live can bear almost any how.”

Friedrich Nietzsche, Twilight of the Idols.

Connections

Existentialism Morality Meaning Culture

Simone de Beauvoir

1908–1986

Who was she?

A French philosopher, writer, and existentialist thinker whose work examined freedom, oppression, gender, ethics, and social structures.

Freedom and society

Beauvoir examined how social conditions can shape the possibilities available to individuals while still asking how people exercise freedom within those conditions.

Major work

The Second Sex became a major work in feminist philosophy and examined how women have historically been constructed as the “Other.”

“Change your life today.”

Simone de Beauvoir, commonly associated quotation.

Schools of thought

TRADITIONS & APPROACHES

A school of thought is not necessarily a perfectly unified group. Philosophers within the same tradition can disagree substantially. Think of these as families of ideas rather than boxes.

Stoicism

Ancient Greece & Rome

Stoicism emphasizes living according to reason and virtue while distinguishing between what is within our control and what is not.


Thinkers: Zeno, Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius.

Epicureanism

Ancient Greece

Epicureanism argues that a good life involves tranquility, friendship, freedom from unnecessary fear, and thoughtful pursuit of pleasure.


Thinker: Epicurus.

Rationalism

Early Modern Europe

Rationalist traditions emphasize reason as a major source of knowledge and investigate what can be established through rational thought.


Thinkers: Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz.

Empiricism

Early Modern Europe

Empiricism emphasizes experience and observation in the formation and justification of knowledge.


Thinkers: Locke, Berkeley, Hume.

Utilitarianism

18th–19th Century

Utilitarian ethics evaluates actions partly by their consequences and seeks to promote overall well-being.


Thinkers: Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill.

Existentialism

19th–20th Century

Existentialist philosophy explores freedom, responsibility, meaning, anxiety, authenticity, and the individual's relationship with an uncertain world.


Thinkers: Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Sartre, Beauvoir.

Pragmatism

United States

Pragmatism examines ideas partly through their practical consequences, uses, and relationship to inquiry and experience.


Thinkers: Peirce, William James, John Dewey.

Deontology

Modern Ethics

Deontological approaches emphasize duties, rules, and moral constraints rather than evaluating morality solely through consequences.


Thinker: Immanuel Kant.

Virtue Ethics

Ancient Greece

Virtue ethics focuses on character and the development of stable dispositions that allow a person to live well.


Thinker: Aristotle.

Ask a question

TURN CURIOSITY INTO INQUIRY

Philosophy begins with questions. The goal is not necessarily to reach an immediate answer, but to understand the strongest arguments, assumptions, objections, and consequences.

Do humans have free will?

Investigate determinism, libertarian free will, compatibilism, and moral responsibility.

What makes an action morally right?

Compare consequences, duties, virtues, intentions, and moral rules.

What makes a government legitimate?

Explore consent, rights, authority, justice, democracy, and political obligation.

Can we know reality as it truly is?

Investigate skepticism, perception, reason, experience, and the limits of human knowledge.

What makes life meaningful?

Compare religious, existentialist, nihilist, humanist, and other approaches to meaning.

What makes a person the same person over time?

Explore memory, consciousness, the body, identity, and psychological continuity.
