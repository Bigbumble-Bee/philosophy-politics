01 — HISTORY

Follow the timeline

Political systems and political ideas developed over thousands of years. Follow the timeline to see how ideas about authority, citizenship, rights, and government changed over time.

c. 3500–500 BCE

Early states and civilizations

Early civilizations developed organized governments, legal systems, taxation, armies, and social hierarchies. Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, and ancient China provide some of the earliest examples of large-scale political organization.

c. 508 BCE

Athenian democracy

Athens developed a form of direct democracy in which eligible citizens participated directly in political decisions. Its system excluded large parts of the population from citizenship.

c. 380 BCE

Plato and the ideal state

Plato examined justice, political leadership, education, and the organization of society in The Republic.

c. 350 BCE

Aristotle studies government

Aristotle compared different forms of government and examined citizenship, constitutions, political stability, and the purpose of the political community.

1215

Magna Carta

The Magna Carta placed important limits on royal authority in medieval England and became influential in the later development of constitutional government and the rule of law.

1600s–1700s

The Enlightenment

Enlightenment thinkers developed influential arguments about natural rights, reason, liberty, sovereignty, government, and the relationship between citizens and political authority.

1776

American Declaration of Independence

The Declaration argued that governments derive their legitimacy from the consent of the governed and that people possess fundamental rights.

1787–1788

Constitutional government

The United States Constitution established a federal system with separated powers, checks and balances, representative government, and a framework for limiting governmental authority.

1789

French Revolution

The French Revolution challenged monarchy and inherited privilege while introducing powerful debates about citizenship, equality, sovereignty, and political rights.

1800s

Industrialization and new ideologies

Industrialization transformed societies and contributed to the development and spread of liberalism, socialism, nationalism, labor movements, and other political ideologies.

1900s

Mass democracy and political movements

The twentieth century saw major struggles over democracy, colonialism, communism, fascism, civil rights, women's political participation, national independence, and human rights.

2000s–present

Politics in a connected world

Digital communication, globalization, political polarization, misinformation, migration, climate politics, and changing relationships between governments and citizens shape contemporary political life.

03 — GOVERNMENT

Forms of government

Government describes the institutions and structures through which political authority is organized and exercised.

PEOPLE

Democracy

Political authority is connected to the people, usually through voting, representation, participation, and constitutional rules.

REPRESENTATION

Republic

A system in which political authority is exercised through representatives and institutions rather than hereditary monarchy.

MONARCH

Monarchy

A system in which a monarch serves as head of state. Monarchies vary greatly in how much political power the monarch actually holds.

SMALL GROUP

Oligarchy

Political power is concentrated among a relatively small group of people.

ONE RULER

Autocracy

Political authority is concentrated in the hands of one ruler or a very small ruling group.

RELIGIOUS AUTHORITY

Theocracy

Governmental authority is strongly connected to religious leadership or religious law.

CENTRALIZED POWER

Authoritarianism

Political power is concentrated and political participation and opposition are significantly restricted.

EXTENSIVE CONTROL

Totalitarianism

A form of political control in which a regime seeks extensive control over political and social life.

04 — IDEAS

Political ideologies

Ideologies are systems of political ideas about how society should be organized and how power, rights, property, equality, and government should work.

LIBERTY

Liberalism

Emphasizes individual liberty, rights, constitutional government, and political equality.

TRADITION

Conservatism

Generally emphasizes tradition, social continuity, institutional stability, and cautious approaches to political change.

ECONOMIC EQUALITY

Socialism

Encompasses traditions that emphasize social or collective control over important economic resources and concerns about economic inequality.

CLASSLESS SOCIETY

Communism

A political and economic tradition associated with common ownership and the goal of a classless society, particularly through Marxist traditions.

NATION

Nationalism

Emphasizes the political importance of the nation and often argues that national identity should have a significant role in political organization.

ANTI-HIERARCHY

Anarchism

Includes traditions that criticize coercive hierarchy and centralized state authority while proposing alternative forms of social organization.

GENDER & POWER

Feminism

A broad collection of movements and theories concerned with gender equality, power, rights, and the social and political position of women.

ENVIRONMENT

Environmentalism

Political movements and ideas focused on the relationship between human societies, environmental systems, and long-term sustainability.

05 — GLOSSARY

Learn the concepts

Political research becomes much easier once its recurring vocabulary becomes familiar.

Sovereignty

The supreme authority to govern a political territory or community.

Legitimacy

The perceived rightfulness or justification of political authority.

Rule of Law

The principle that laws govern public authority and that government officials are also subject to the law.

Separation of Powers

Dividing governmental authority among different branches or institutions to prevent excessive concentration of power.

Checks and Balances

Mechanisms that allow different parts of government to limit or oversee one another.

Federalism

A system in which governmental authority is constitutionally divided between national and subnational governments.

Popular Sovereignty

The principle that political authority ultimately comes from the people.

Social Contract

A family of political theories explaining political authority through some form of agreement, consent, or mutual justification among people.

Civil Liberties

Protections against governmental interference with fundamental freedoms.

Civil Rights

Legal protections intended to secure equal treatment and prevent discrimination.

Political Participation

The ways people take part in political life, including voting, organizing, advocacy, and public discussion.

Political Power

The ability to influence political decisions, institutions, behavior, or the distribution of resources.

06 — PRIMARY SOURCES

Documents worth knowing

Political ideas become much more useful when you encounter them in the original documents that shaped political history.

1215

Magna Carta

An influential medieval charter associated with limits on royal authority and the development of constitutional ideas.

1689

English Bill of Rights

Established important limits on the English monarchy and protections connected to Parliament and political rights.

1776

Declaration of Independence

Presented an argument for independence based on natural rights, equality, and government by consent.

1787

United States Constitution

Established the framework of the United States federal government and its institutional structure.

1787–1788

The Federalist Papers

Essays defending and explaining the proposed United States Constitution, including arguments about factions, federalism, representation, and checks and balances.

1948

Universal Declaration of Human Rights

A foundational international statement describing fundamental human rights and freedoms.

07 — INQUIRY

Ask a political question

Good political research usually begins with a good question. Start with something you genuinely want to understand.

Your question

Start here:
  1. Define the important terms in your question.
  2. Find at least two thinkers or political traditions that approach the issue differently.
  3. Identify the strongest argument on each side.
  4. Look for historical examples that support or challenge those arguments.
  5. Write your own provisional conclusion.
